About the trip

In July 2026, a small Global Food Institute team spent a week in Haiti with the partners behind the Crisis-Resilient School Meals pilot, which aims to put food grown by Haitian farmers into Haitian school meals.

We followed that food from the field to the school kitchen across the Central Plateau, then joined the partners for two days of planning in Caracol. This is what we saw, and what comes next.

Haiti in the Caribbean

Haiti shares the island of Hispaniola with the Dominican Republic, between Cuba and Puerto Rico. Cap-Haïtien, where the team landed, is about 2,200 km (1,350 miles) from George Washington University in Washington, D.C.

One week across northern Haiti

This past July a small contingent from the Global Food Institute took a week to go to Haiti in order to meet with partners in person who are working in country. The work trip was aimed at meetings and visiting project sites across the Central Plateau, then closing out the week with a conference that brought representatives from six different contributing organizations together to review progress and coordinate on what the next steps of the project look like.

What we took away

The route showed us each link in the chain that carries Haitian harvests to school meals: the farmers who grow the food, the depot that gathers it, the kitchens that cook it, and the partners working to make it last. These are the moments that stayed with us.

Into the Central Plateau

Days 1–3, Centre

This trip started in the north at the city of Cap-Haïtien, where we landed. After a quick breakfast of Haitian spaghetti we hit the road towards the Central Plateau. After a scenic, bumpy four hours we arrived in Hinche, a city in the Central Plateau. This hotel was our home base for the next two days while we were visiting different sites in the area; the local Acceso depot and two different schools that were a part of the Summits program.

We met with local farmers who were working with Acceso and discussed how and why they like this program. This meeting was very helpful in better understanding the supply chain from smallholder farmer to school children's meals. We also got to see part of the process ourselves and hear firsthand about what is going on and what can be improved.

From farm to depot

Thomonde, Acceso depot, Aggregation

In storage during our visit Black beans Peanuts

This is the Acceso depot for the area. Acceso runs depots across the country wherever it works. Each one is a collection point for local farmers: their harvests are pooled here, then shipped to wherever the order is going, whether that's a market, a school, or another buyer.

Two schools

Summits Education program, School meals

Our first school visit, to École Communautaire Saint Paul in Morne Thomonde, happened to coincide with a teacher training seminar. Compared with other schools in the region, this one was relatively well built, though it still faced its share of challenges. Staff emphasized how much school meals matter. For many families, a guaranteed meal is a strong reason to send children to school, and students who aren't hungry are better able to concentrate and learn.

The second school, École Communautaire Nouvelle Génération in Mogé, was a sharp contrast to the first. It had far less infrastructure, but the site was busy with major construction. Once finished, the new buildings will make room for more students and give the school modern spaces for storing and preparing food, a big step toward making daily meals more reliable.

Much of the school's growth is thanks to the man standing in front. A member of the local community, he donated his own land so the school could expand. Education matters deeply to him, and he took it upon himself to help build a campus where students can learn and count on a daily meal.

Meds & Food for Kids

Outside Cap-Haïtien, Nord, Processing

Common thread The same crop we saw stacked at the Acceso depot, the peanut, is the base of Meds & Food for Kids' main product.

Meds & Food for Kids was founded in 2003 by pediatrician Dr. Patricia Wolff to fight child malnutrition in Haiti. Its main products are peanut-based therapeutic foods that need no refrigeration or clean water to use. Its factory outside Cap-Haïtien opened in 2012. It produces all the therapeutic food used in Haiti and ships to 14 other countries through UNICEF. Run by a team of nearly 100 and supported by the local farmers it trains, the organization has treated more than one million children and mothers.

The conference

Caracol, Nord-Est, 2 days

The conference brought the partner organizations together for two days of meetings and breakout sessions. We reviewed the work done so far, but most of the time went to moving the project forward and agreeing on next steps.

What comes next

The pilot runs for two school years, with the aim of serving locally sourced meals to about 48,000 students. Alongside the meals, the Global Food Institute and Chibas are building the evidence for whether the model works: mapping how food, money, and decisions move across the Central Plateau, working with farmers, cooks, and community members as co-researchers, and developing early-warning tools that flag supply disruptions, price spikes, or falling meal attendance before they turn into a crisis.

The first results are already being published.

  • From Aid to Engine: Reimagining School Feeding as a Driver of Resilient Food SystemsGlobal Food Institute, August 2026. The first in a series on the School Meals 3.0 model, also available in French.
  • Research in Practice: Deploying Anticipatory Analytics for School Feeding Programs in Fragile SettingsGlobal Food Institute and Summits Education, September 2026. How routine school feeding data can warn of trouble ahead.

The partners behind the pilot

Farmers & markets

Acceso

Supports smallholder farmers through technical assistance, financing, and market access, helping them grow more, sell more, and earn more while connecting local supply to school feeding programs and other buyers across Haiti.

School meals

Summits Education

Provides and improves daily local meal delivery for schoolchildren across the Central Plateau, including by developing a Resilience Hub to expand school-level capacity.

Crisis response

World Central Kitchen

Contributes expertise in crisis response, along with investments and operational insight that strengthen the ability to prepare for and respond to anything that may happen.

National network

Haiti Food System Alliance

Connects this project to Haiti's broader food system through its network of local members, creating the links needed to take what works beyond the Central Plateau.

Analytic partner

George Washington University's Global Food Institute

Provides the research design, data tools, and learning systems that measure how the pilot performs, let it adapt in real time, and build the case for taking it to scale.

Haitian research partner

Chibas

Works alongside the Global Food Institute, bringing deep local expertise. Chibas leads field and agricultural research and makes sure the findings reflect what communities actually experience and prioritize.

The Crisis-Resilient School Meals pilot is funded by the Longer Tables Fund and The Rockefeller Foundation. Read more about the project at the Global Food Institute.
